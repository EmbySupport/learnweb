    Progressive Video

    Progressive video represents each picture as a complete frame. A 1080p frame contains the full 1080-line picture, rather than combining alternating lines captured at two successive field times.

    The letter p identifies progressive scanning. In “1080p24,” 1080 describes the picture height and 24 describes the frame rate. Neither value tells you the bitrate, color format, or quality of the compression.

    Progressive example Complete pictures per second Picture height
    1080p24 24 1080 lines
    1080p50 50 1080 lines
    2160p60 60 2160 lines

    These examples use integer rates; corresponding fractional-rate formats also exist.

    Complete frames still sample time

    Progressive does not mean motion is continuous. Each captured picture comes from an exposure, and the next picture follows later. A low frame rate can produce visible steps during a pan; a long exposure can blur movement within one frame. Both effects can occur in entirely valid progressive video.

    For Emby playback, a progressive source can be decoded and scaled without reconstructing missing lines from alternate capture times. That simplifies the presentation path, but does not remove the need for compatible codecs, correct timing, or suitable display processing.

    There is one useful wrinkle in older studio material: progressive segmented frame (PsF) transport splits a progressive picture into two field-shaped segments. Both segments belong to the same captured picture. That differs from native interlaced video, whose alternating fields describe successive times.

    Consequently, the presence of field-like transport or an interlace flag does not by itself prove that the original scene was captured with interlaced motion. Inspect moving content and the source's signaling together. Unnecessary deinterlacing can alter an otherwise complete progressive image.

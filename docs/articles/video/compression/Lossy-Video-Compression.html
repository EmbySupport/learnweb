    Lossy Video Compression

    Lossy video compression permits the decoded picture to differ from the picture supplied to the encoder. The aim is to reduce data while keeping the differences acceptable for the intended viewing conditions.

    In many modern codecs, an encoder predicts part of the picture, transforms the remaining difference, and quantizes the resulting values. Quantization approximates values using a chosen set of representable steps. Small differences can become zero, leaving less information to encode.

    Value before approximation Rounded to the nearest multiple of eight Difference
    17 16 −1
    −3 0 +3
    39 40 +1

    Original scalar example illustrating irreversible rounding. These are not pixel measurements or the quantization equations of a particular codec.

    The decoder cannot know whether a decoded zero originally came from −3, +2, or exactly zero. That information has been discarded. The file can still look indistinguishable in ordinary viewing: “lossy” describes mathematical reconstruction, not a guarantee of visible damage.

    What becomes noticeable first?

    Fine texture, grain, gradients, and fast movement can expose compromises differently. Too little data may soften detail, break up moving regions, or make smooth shading appear stepped. A bitrate that suits a quiet dialogue scene may be insufficient for dense grain or rapid action.

    Most films in an Emby library already use lossy video compression. Direct Play preserves that encoded video stream during delivery. Video transcoding creates another encoded representation; when that encoding is lossy, it can add differences on top of the original ones.

    Increasing the output bitrate cannot restore detail lost earlier. It can help the new encoding avoid further visible damage, but a larger converted file is not evidence that the picture became closer to the camera original.

    A good comparison therefore considers the actual pictures, playback conditions, and delivery requirements alongside the file size.

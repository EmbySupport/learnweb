    Video Codec Levels and Tiers

    A codec level describes limits on the workload and buffering required by a coded stream. A tier, in codecs that define one, distinguishes further limits within a level, particularly bitrate and coded-buffer requirements.

    A profile tells the decoder which kinds of tools and sample formats it may encounter. Level and tier address how much of that work it must handle.

    Constraint Why it matters
    Picture size A decoder must reconstruct and store the complete picture
    Samples or coding units per second Larger pictures and higher frame rates multiply throughput
    Decoded picture buffer References and reordered pictures consume memory
    Bitrate and coded buffering The compressed data must be accepted at the allowed rate

    A level is not one resolution

    A stream with smaller pictures at a high frame rate can reach a throughput limit that a larger, slower stream does not. Picture dimensions alone therefore do not establish conformance. Bitrate and buffer requirements can matter even when the resolution is familiar.

    The numbers are also codec-specific. H.264 Level 4.1 and HEVC Level 4.1 do not describe the same limits, and the profile can affect limits within a codec. Avoid universal tables that assign one maximum resolution or bitrate to every codec's level number.

    HEVC defines Main and High tiers. High tier permits larger bitrate and coded-buffer limits where that tier is defined; it does not automatically improve the encoded picture. A string such as “Main 10, Level 5.1, Main tier” combines three separate properties.

    For an Emby app to decode a stream, its playback path must meet the actual requirements. A device advertised for a particular resolution may also limit frame rate, profile, tier, or concurrent decoder workload.

    Lowering a level flag cannot make an oversized or excessive-throughput stream conform. Conversely, a stream may be tagged with a higher level than its pictures actually require. Establishing that the label can safely change requires checking the full bitstream requirements, rather than guessing from resolution.

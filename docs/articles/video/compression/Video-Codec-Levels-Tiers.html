    Video Codec Levels and Tiers

    A codec level defines a resource envelope for a bitstream: how large its coded pictures can be, how quickly they must be processed, how much picture state can be retained, and how much coded data must be buffered.

    A profile selects the kinds of operations. Level limits describe the scale at which those operations are required. Some codecs also define tiers, particularly for bitrate and coded-buffer limits.

    Work through a 1080p AVC stream

    H.264 counts several level resources in 16 × 16 macroblocks. For a progressive picture presented as 1920 × 1080, round each coded grid dimension up to a macroblock boundary:

    • Width: 1920/16 = 120 macroblocks.
    • Height: ceil(1080/16) = 68 macroblocks.
    • Picture size: 120 × 68 = 8,160 macroblocks.

    The coded macroblock grid is 1920 × 1088. A signaled crop can remove the extra eight rows from presentation. The decoder's coded-picture work still follows the larger macroblock grid.

    For H.264 Level 4.1, selected limits are:

    Resource Level 4.1 limit
    Macroblocks in a coded frame, MaxFS 8,192
    Macroblocks processed per second, MaxMBPS 245,760
    Decoded-picture-buffer macroblocks, MaxDpbMbs 32,768

    These are selected standard constraints, not the whole conformance test.

    Picture size and throughput give different answers

    The example's 8,160 macroblocks fit the per-picture limit of 8,192.

    At 30 fps, processing demand is:

    8,160 × 30 = 244,800 macroblocks/s, below 245,760.

    At 60 fps, demand is:

    8,160 × 60 = 489,600 macroblocks/s, above that limit.

    The dimensions are identical, but the second stream requires a larger throughput envelope. A label assigning “1080p” to one universal level would hide this difference.

    Other level constraints still need checking, including bitrate, coded buffering, motion limits, and profile-dependent factors. Passing the two calculations above explains one part of the requirement rather than certifying an arbitrary file.

    The reference-buffer calculation

    At this coded picture size, the selected DPB limit gives:

    floor(32,768 ÷ 8,160) = 4 picture slots.

    References and pictures awaiting output have to satisfy the decoded-buffer rules. A stream requesting more state cannot be made compliant by reducing only its bitrate. Conversely, a small compressed file can still require the full decoded picture surfaces.

    The reference-buffer article explains those surfaces' lifetimes and sample-memory footprints. A standard's logical DPB constraint and an implementation's total allocated memory are related but distinct measurements.

    Read a tier with its codec

    HEVC defines Main and High tiers. Where High tier is defined, it permits higher bitrate and coded-buffer limits within the relevant level. It does not select a different camera resolution or automatically improve the picture.

    In the description “HEVC Main 10, Level 5.1, Main tier”:

    • Main 10 is the profile's sample/tool permission.
    • 5.1 is the level's resource envelope.
    • Main tier selects the corresponding bitrate/buffer limits.

    The word “Main” serves two different names in this family. The entire codec-specific description supplies the meaning; H.264 Level 4.1 and HEVC Level 4.1 do not share one interchangeable resource table.

    Change the demand, or correct an excessive declaration

    If a source really exceeds a target's throughput, producing a suitable representation can require a lower picture rate, smaller raster, different reference structure, or other encoding changes. Editing its level flag does not reduce the coded work.

    A file can also be declared at a higher level than its actual demand needs. Correcting such a declaration requires checking the complete applicable constraints, including peak and buffer behavior, rather than looking only at the visible resolution.

    For an Emby decoder target, these calculations turn a broad resolution claim into questions about the actual stream: its coded grid, picture rate, retained state, profile, tier, and delivery demand.

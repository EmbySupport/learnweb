    Constant Rate Factor (CRF) for Video

    Constant rate factor (CRF) is a quality-control method used by encoders such as x264 and x265. It allows bitrate to vary while adjusting quantization and other decisions around an encoder-defined quality target.

    A lower CRF in these encoders generally means finer quantization and a larger result; a higher value generally means stronger compression. CRF does not specify a target bitrate or exact output size.

    Change within the same encoding setup Typical consequence
    Lower CRF More data to preserve the source
    Higher CRF Less data and greater compression compromises
    More demanding source at the same CRF A larger result
    Add a restrictive rate cap The quality target may no longer be maintained

    Directions, not measured results or a guarantee for every individual encode.

    The number belongs to the encoder

    CRF 20 in x264 and CRF 20 in x265 are not equivalent quality levels. An AV1 encoder's CRF-like control uses its own model and scale. Compare settings within the same encoder, version, preset, and sample format before drawing conclusions from the number.

    CRF is also different from constant QP. In CRF mode, the quantizers can change with the pictures and the encoder's analysis. The word “constant” refers to the chosen rate factor, not a fixed quantizer or identical visible quality.

    Do not interpret zero as a universal lossless switch. Encoders have different lossless conventions; x265, for example, documents an explicit lossless mode. A low CRF is not by itself proof of exact sample preservation.

    For an Emby source file, CRF explains how the file was encoded, not how it will be delivered to every app. A playback limit may still trigger conversion. A rate cap and buffer constraint can help control an encode's delivery demand, but they introduce a separate tradeoff when difficult scenes reach that cap.

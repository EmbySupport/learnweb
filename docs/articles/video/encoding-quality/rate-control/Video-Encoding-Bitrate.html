    Video Bitrate

    Video bitrate describes the amount of coded picture data per second. It excludes the soundtrack and container overhead. When choosing an encode, the number can be a target average, a local rate limit, or the measured result of a quality-based encode; those meanings should not be mixed.

    A bitrate is a data budget, not a picture-quality score. Its effect depends on the source, codec, resolution, frame rate, and the encoder's decisions.

    Illustrative video rate Data for one second Video-only size for two hours
    4 Mb/s 0.5 MB 3.6 GB
    8 Mb/s 1 MB 7.2 GB
    16 Mb/s 2 MB 14.4 GB

    Decimal units and an exact average over 7,200 seconds. Audio and packaging are excluded; these are size calculations, not recommended quality settings.

    What must fit into that budget?

    At the same bitrate, doubling the frame rate halves the average number of bits available per picture. It does not follow that the rate must always double to preserve quality: neighboring pictures can share predictable information. Dense grain or complicated motion may be much harder to compress than a quiet scene.

    A slower preset can give an encoder more time to use its tools. That can improve the result within a fixed budget, but it does not add missing source detail or guarantee a particular quality.

    In Emby, compare the library video's bitrate with the delivered stream when video conversion occurs. The playback quality setting can constrain delivery; it is not a measurement of how faithfully every scene will survive that constraint.

    For storage, average bitrate is useful. For playback, short demanding periods and the available buffer matter too. Treat a video's reported average as one part of the delivery requirement, rather than assuming the connection needs exactly that rate at every instant.

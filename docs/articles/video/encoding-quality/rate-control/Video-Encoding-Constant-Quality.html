    Constant Quality Video Encoding

    Constant quality encoding starts with a quality target and lets the encoder use different amounts of data as the source changes. The target is defined by the encoder's method; it is not a guarantee that every scene looks equally good to every viewer.

    This is useful when creating a library copy whose acceptable appearance matters more than meeting an exact file size. A clean animation and a grainy live-action recording can produce very different sizes at the same setting.

    What you choose What is mostly allowed to vary
    An encoder-specific quality target The resulting bitrate and size
    A fixed quantizer mode The picture error and bitrate as content changes
    A quality target with a rate cap Bitrate, and potentially quality when the cap binds

    The methods are related but not interchangeable. CRF adapts encoding decisions around a rate factor. Constant QP holds a quantization setting according to its implementation. Some hardware encoders offer their own target-quality modes.

    A cap creates another constraint

    A quality target can be combined with a maximum rate and a buffer size. When the encoder cannot meet both the unconstrained quality target and the rate constraint, it must compromise. A difficult section may therefore look worse than the target would suggest.

    The numerical scales also differ. A quality value in one software encoder, hardware API, or codec should not be copied into another and assumed to represent the same result.

    For an Emby library, a quality-controlled source can still require conversion because its peaks, format, or profile exceed what the app can accept. Check the delivered stream before concluding that the library encode is responsible for a visible change.

    Use representative difficult scenes when assessing a setting: grain, dark gradients, fast movement, and fine texture reveal different compromises. The eventual file size is an outcome of that evaluation, not proof of quality on its own.

    Structural Similarity (SSIM) for Video

    Structural Similarity (SSIM) compares the local statistical structure of two pictures. It considers more than pixel-by-pixel error, aiming to reflect the importance of recognizable patterns and edges.

    In video evaluation, one sequence is the reference and the other is the test result. Corresponding decoded pictures are compared, often through small windows, before results are combined into an overall score.

    Part of the original SSIM comparison What it describes
    Luminance Local average sample value
    Contrast Variation around the local average
    Structure How the local variations correspond

    A blurred texture and a small uniform brightness change can have similar squared error but affect these relationships differently. SSIM can therefore distinguish cases that PSNR treats as equal. This does not make it a perfect predictor of what every viewer prefers.

    Read the implementation as well as the score

    A value of 1 represents identical compared content in the original formulation. SSIM is unitless, and negative values are possible; it is not universally confined to 0–1. A result such as 0.99 does not mean “99% picture quality.”

    Single-scale SSIM, multi-scale variants, and different implementations need not produce interchangeable numbers. Windowing, color handling, downsampling, and aggregation all matter. The reference implementation's suggested scaling is one reason tools can disagree even when using the same videos.

    For an Emby library conversion, compare candidate encodes against the same retained source with consistent timing, dimensions, and sample preparation. A different crop or one-frame offset can measure a mismatch rather than the effect of the encoder.

    Inspect difficult scenes in motion alongside the aggregate result. A whole-film score can hide a short bad section, and an image-similarity measurement does not describe network stalls or playback compatibility. SSIM helps evaluate the reconstruction; it does not prove the source was good or that the output retained every original sample.

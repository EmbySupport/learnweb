    Color Volume

    Color volume adds light level to color gamut. It asks which colors a display can reproduce from its darkest output to its brightest, rather than describing only the reach of its red, green, and blue primaries.

    Consider two imaginary screens that can produce the same saturated red at a moderate light level. One can keep that red saturated as it becomes brighter; the other must reduce its brightness or move it toward white. Their gamut coverage at the tested level can look similar, while their handling of bright colored objects differs.

    Picture detail What matters
    A deep red costume in a dim room Low-light color and shadow separation
    A red traffic signal at night Saturation at a higher luminance
    A white reflection on chrome Peak white and highlight handling
    A bright orange flame Color and luminance together

    This is why a peak-brightness number alone cannot describe HDR picture quality. A white patch and a saturated color ask different things of a display. Test-window size and duration also matter when interpreting brightness measurements; one brief small highlight does not describe a whole bright scene.

    From the mastering display to yours

    HDR mastering-display metadata records a display's primaries, white point, and luminance limits. It describes the mastering reference, not every color actually used in the film and not the capability of your television.

    During Emby playback, the file can therefore describe a larger volume than the output display can reproduce. Tone mapping and gamut mapping address related parts of this problem. Preserving highlight brightness while ignoring saturation can still change the appearance of a flame or neon sign.

    For comparisons, look for measured performance across brightness and color, with a stated test method. A single percentage without its reference volume and measurement conditions is hard to interpret.

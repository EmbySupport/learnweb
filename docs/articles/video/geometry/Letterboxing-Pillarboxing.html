    Letterboxing and Pillarboxing

    Black bars usually mean that the picture and the screen have different proportions. Letterboxing leaves space above and below a wider picture. Pillarboxing leaves space to the left and right of a narrower one.

    Original proportional examples on 16:9 screens: a 2.4:1 film at left and a 4:3 picture at right. Green marks the active image.

    The bars preserve the entire picture at its intended aspect ratio. Removing them by zooming requires part of the picture to leave the screen. Removing them by stretching changes the shapes inside it.

    Are the bars in the file?

    Where the bars come from What the file contains Consequence
    Added during display Only the narrower or wider picture Their size can change with the screen or player window
    Baked into the video Picture samples plus dark border samples The bars remain part of the decoded frame

    A 1920 × 800 film can gain bars when fitted to a 1920 × 1080 display. Another copy may already contain those bars in a 1920 × 1080 frame. Both can look identical on that display, yet behave differently when fitted to an ultrawide screen.

    For Emby playback, this distinction helps explain why selecting a fill or zoom mode sometimes still leaves bars. The player may be fitting the whole stored frame, including its baked-in borders. Cropping can remove those samples, but it should be based on the actual content.

    Films can change aspect ratio between scenes, and subtitles may occupy a border. A crop that looks right at the beginning can cut off material later. Empty space is often the correct way to preserve a composition, rather than evidence that playback quality is too low.

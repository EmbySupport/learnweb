    HDR-to-HDR Tone Mapping

    An HDR screen can still need tone mapping. Accepting an HDR signal tells us which signal formats the screen understands; it does not mean the screen can reproduce every brightness that signal can describe.

    Consider a film containing highlights at 2,000 nits played on a display that can reach 600 nits under the relevant conditions. Both ends of this example are HDR. The display still needs to fit those highlights into its available range. HDR-to-HDR tone mapping does that without first reducing the picture to SDR.

    Part of the picture What a suitable mapping aims to do
    Ordinary midtones Keep their intended appearance where the target permits
    Bright highlights Compress their range while retaining visible differences
    Dark detail Account for the target's black level rather than assuming perfect black

    These are goals, not a guarantee that every mapping leaves midtones unchanged.

    HDR describes more than one display capability

    PQ describes absolute display-light levels over a much larger range than a typical consumer screen can reproduce. The BT.2390 report describes adapting those levels to a target with different black and white limits. The output can remain an HDR signal while its useful brightness range changes.

    The source's mastering-display maximum is not necessarily its brightest actual pixel. Content analysis and metadata help establish what needs fitting. Color volume also matters: retaining HDR does not remove limits on bright, saturated colors.

    For Emby playback, separate the delivered stream from the screen's final rendering. With Direct Play, the server has not rewritten the video, but the player or display can still adapt it. Two HDR televisions can therefore show the same Emby stream differently. The useful starting point is where the mapping happens, before comparing curves or changing server settings.

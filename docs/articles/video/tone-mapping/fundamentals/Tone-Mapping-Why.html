    Why Tone Mapping Is Needed

    An HDR film can contain a bright reflection on a car alongside a face in shadow. An SDR screen cannot reproduce the same range of light. Tone mapping fits that range into the available output, deciding how much room to give the face, the reflection, and everything between them.

    Simply cutting off everything above the screen's limit loses differences between bright objects. Darkening the entire picture can fit the highlights, but make the rest unnecessarily dim. A tone-mapping curve makes a more selective compromise.

    For scale, an SDR reference viewing setup uses a peak of 100 nits, while an HDR highlight might reach 1,000 nits. A nit is a unit of luminance (one candela per square metre). Dividing every light level by ten would fit that example, but would also turn a 100-nit object into a 10-nit one.

    Why changing the playback quality is not enough

    Change What it changes Does it solve the brightness mismatch?
    Lower the resolution The number of pixels No
    Reduce the bitrate The amount of compressed data No
    Relabel HDR as SDR How a player interprets the signal No; the picture values still need conversion
    Tone-map the picture The relationship between input and output brightness Yes, as part of a correctly configured conversion

    Tone mapping is one part of HDR-to-SDR conversion. Color also needs appropriate handling; fixing brightness alone does not guarantee correct color.

    Where Emby fits

    The work can happen at different points in a playback session. Emby Server can tone-map during video transcoding. A player can also do it locally: the Emby Windows FAQ describes tone mapping in the app's rendering stage.

    That is why the server's playback method and the final appearance are separate questions. Direct Play describes delivery from the server; it does not mean the player or display leaves the picture untouched.

    Different mappings can retain different amounts of highlight contrast or change the apparent brightness of faces. They cannot reproduce all the original HDR brightness on an SDR screen, or guarantee the same result as a separately graded SDR release.

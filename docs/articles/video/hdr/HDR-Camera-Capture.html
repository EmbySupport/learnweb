    HDR Capture in Cameras

    An HDR picture starts with information the camera actually captured. If a sunlit window has already become a featureless clipped patch in the recording, an HDR export label cannot bring its detail back.

    Capture dynamic range is often described in stops. Each additional stop represents a doubling of the light ratio. This describes the sensor and recording workflow; it is not a promise about how many nits the finished film will use.

    A useful distinction for home videos in an Emby library is whether the recording is ready for normal viewing or still needs production work.

    Recording approach What happens next
    Log video A deliberately encoded source for grading and conversion into a viewing format
    Raw capture Sensor-oriented data that needs development and an image-processing pipeline
    HLG recording Can provide a delivery-ready HDR signal for a compatible viewing path

    A flat-looking log recording is therefore not necessarily broken HDR. Sony, for example, describes S-Log as intended for subsequent grading, while HLG recordings are intended for compatible HDR displays. Camera preview assistance can show a more normal-looking image without changing the underlying log recording.

    Exposure still matters. Preserving a bright sky by lowering exposure too far can leave important shadows noisy. HDR can make both clipped highlights and shadow noise more visible, because the final presentation has room to reveal them.

    For a personal-video library, identify the recording mode before diagnosing playback. Grading and mastering turn captured material into a finished image. HDR-to-SDR tone mapping solves a later display-range problem; it is not a substitute for developing or grading camera originals.

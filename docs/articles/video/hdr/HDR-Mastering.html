    HDR Mastering in the Studio

    HDR mastering is where the available camera information becomes a deliberate viewing experience. The colorist decides how bright a window should feel, whether faces remain restrained, and which shadow details should be visible.

    A camera may capture more information than the finished release uses. Mastering is not an instruction to stretch every shot from black to maximum brightness.

    Production decision What the viewer may notice
    Highlight placement A reflection can sparkle without the whole scene becoming bright
    Shot matching Cuts between cameras keep a coherent look
    Shadow treatment A night scene can remain dark while important objects remain distinguishable
    Mapping review The image is checked on targets with less brightness or a different gamut

    The reference monitor gives those judgments a known basis. Mastering display metadata describes its configured color volume; it does not describe every pixel in the movie or require a home screen with identical capabilities.

    For Dolby Vision, image analysis can be followed by a trim pass: the colorist reviews a mapped version and, where needed, adjusts its appearance for another target. Those decisions can travel as metadata rather than as a complete second movie.

    This helps explain a familiar Emby comparison: an HDR release converted to SDR during playback need not look identical to a separately mastered SDR release. The two may incorporate different creative decisions, even if both are technically correct.

    When judging a tone-mapping result, look for lost detail or unnatural shifts within the same release first. Treat the separate SDR release as another authored version, not a pixel-for-pixel reference that every HDR conversion must reproduce.

    MaxCLL and MaxFALL

    MaxCLL and MaxFALL describe different kinds of brightness in a video. Both are expressed in nits, but one concerns the brightest content and the other the brightest frame average.

    Field Expanded name What it summarizes
    MaxCLL Maximum Content Light Level The highest content light level across the program
    MaxFALL Maximum Frame-Average Light Level The largest of the individual frame averages

    MaxFALL is not the average brightness of the whole film. Imagine a mostly dark movie with one very bright outdoor shot. That shot can determine MaxFALL even though the rest of the movie is much darker.

    A simplified example shows why the two values differ. If 1% of a frame is at 1,000 nits and the remaining 99% is at 10 nits, its average is 19.9 nits. A bright point can therefore coexist with a low frame average. This arithmetic illustration is not a complete specification of how production tools calculate the metadata.

    These fields are separate from the mastering display's maximum. One describes the content; the other describes a display configuration. Neither number measures the output of the television currently playing the file.

    Missing or zero light-level fields should not be read as a black movie. They can indicate unavailable information. Metadata may also be inaccurate, so a converter can need its own image analysis instead of relying only on the reported maximum.

    When comparing Emby tone-mapping results, record these values alongside the exact source version. Different encodes of the same title can carry different metadata, and that can change an implementation's starting assumptions.

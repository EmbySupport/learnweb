    What HDR Video Adds to SDR

    HDR video gives filmmakers more room to separate bright highlights, ordinary scene content, and dark detail.

    Picture a dim room with a face beside a window and a reflection on glass. In the finished image, the face should remain readable, the window should feel brighter, and the reflection can be brighter still. HDR gives the filmmaker more room to separate those light levels in one picture.

    A conventional SDR rendition has a smaller reference range. Its production or conversion must fit bright areas closer together, choose which differences to compress, and establish a coherent image within that range. HDR can retain more of the separation, so a reflection stands out without requiring the whole room to become bright.

    What changes in the picture?

    Part of the scene Use of the additional range
    Face and ordinary surfaces Can remain at a deliberately chosen comfortable level
    Bright window Can stay distinct from the interior
    Small reflection Can stand out above nearby bright surfaces
    Dark folds and corners Can retain useful low-level distinctions in an appropriate presentation

    The creative choice comes first. A night scene can remain dark, a gray day can remain muted, and a deliberately flat shot can remain low in contrast. The extra range is available room for the image, rather than an instruction to fill every shot from black to the highest supported light level.

    Dynamic range has its own place beside resolution and color

    Resolution determines how many samples describe the scene. Bit depth determines the possible numerical precision of the signal. Gamut determines its color-coordinate reach. HDR's light relationship can improve alongside those properties, but each answers a different question.

    For example, a 4K SDR video can contain fine spatial detail while still using an SDR light representation. A 1080p HDR picture can use a wider light range with fewer spatial samples. A ten-bit file provides more levels than an eight-bit file, while its transfer characteristic determines whether those levels describe SDR, PQ, HLG, or another representation.

    BT.2100 specifies HDR television image parameters. PQ and HLG establish different HDR light relationships. Delivery formats add their particular packaging and metadata conventions.

    Rendering HDR on displays with different ranges

    The source describes a produced image. A receiving display has a usable light range and color volume of its own, and the room affects the appearance. When source requests exceed the destination, tone mapping fits the picture while deciding where to retain contrast.

    An Emby server can deliver HDR unchanged for a compatible playback path or produce a converted rendition for the destination. Local presentation can also adapt an unchanged source to the screen. The playback method identifies server delivery, while the app's output and display mode identify the later presentation.

    To compare HDR results, keep the same source release and scene, then inspect ordinary objects, dark detail, and bright texture together. That evaluates how the image uses its range, rather than judging only the largest brightness number.

    Digital Audio Sampling

    A microphone produces a changing electrical signal. Sampling measures that signal at regular instants, turning its movement through time into a sequence of numbers. Sound created digitally can produce the same sort of sequence without a microphone.

    The time spacing is set by the sample rate. At 48,000 samples per second, neighboring samples in one channel are about 20.8 microseconds apart. The sample's numerical precision is a separate choice: bit depth.

    Calculated example: a 1 kHz sine wave sampled at 8 kHz, shown over two milliseconds. The continuous line is the original mathematical signal; the dots are its sample values. The reduced rate keeps the sample positions visible.

    The gaps are not missing pieces of sound

    Those dots do not instruct a loudspeaker to jump from one flat step to the next. Reconstruction uses the sequence to form a continuous waveform. For a signal limited to frequencies below half the sample rate, ideal reconstruction can recover the waveform from its samples.

    Real recording systems filter out frequencies that cannot be represented before sampling. Without that filtering, a high-frequency component can masquerade as a lower one: aliasing. Filters also matter when changing an existing digital signal's sample rate.

    A stereo sample frame contains one sample for the left channel and one for the right at the same time position. “48 kHz stereo” therefore means 48,000 such frames per second, not 24,000 for each side.

    When an Emby audio track shows a sample rate, it describes this time grid. Compressed audio may store blocks of coded information rather than a visible list of sample values, but decoding still produces audio on a sample timeline.

    Audio Dynamic Range

    A whisper followed by an explosion can be difficult to hear comfortably at one volume setting. That is an example of a recording using a wide dynamic range: quiet and loud passages are far apart.

    The same term also describes a system's usable range, from its noise floor to the largest signal it can handle cleanly. A recording's level variation and a device's capacity are related, but they are different quantities.

    Use of “dynamic range” What is being compared
    A soundtrack's dynamics Quiet and loud parts of the programme
    A converter's usable range Noise floor and maximum clean signal
    Crest factor Peak amplitude and RMS amplitude over the same interval

    Crest factor is useful for describing peaks, but does not by itself measure the difference between a quiet scene and a loud one. Measurements also depend on the chosen interval and method; a single unlabeled “DR” number can be ambiguous.

    Decibels describe a ratio

    For comparable signal amplitudes, the level difference is 20 × log10(larger / smaller). An amplitude ratio of 1,000 to 1 gives 60 dB. This is a calculated ratio, not an absolute listening volume. The sound pressure in the room also depends on the playback gain and the speakers.

    More integer bit depth can lower the quantization limit, but it does not force a soundtrack to use wider dynamics. A highly compressed mix can occupy 24-bit storage just as easily as an uncompressed musical performance.

    When watching through Emby at night, lowering the volume reduces loud and quiet parts together. Processing that narrows the level difference can make quiet passages easier to hear without increasing peaks as much. Its availability and behavior depend on the actual player, decoder, and output system. The source track's bitrate or channel count does not tell you whether that processing is active.

    Audio Clipping

    Clipping occurs when a signal asks for more amplitude than a stage can represent or reproduce. Peaks are cut off instead of following their intended shape. This changes the waveform and can introduce audible distortion.

    Calculated example: a sine with peak amplitude 1.3 is hard-clipped to a normalized range of −1 to +1. The dashed line shows the requested signal; the solid line shows the clipped result. This is a mathematical example, not a measurement of Emby output.

    A signal already close to full scale can exceed the limit when gain is added or several channels are summed. Individual input channels can be valid while their combined output is too large.

    Where the limit applies

    Stage What its range means
    Integer PCM Values must fit the selected integer representation
    Floating-point processing Intermediate values may exceed nominal full scale
    Final output Values must fit the converter and playback chain's available range

    Floating point can preserve an over-range intermediate result so that gain can be reduced before output. It does not give a loudspeaker unlimited headroom. For FFmpeg floating-point audio, −1 to +1 is the nominal full-volume range.

    Turning the volume down after clipping makes the distorted waveform quieter. It cannot recover the cut-off shape. Reducing gain before the overloaded stage can prevent clipping, which is why the position of a volume or boost control matters.

    If an Emby soundtrack distorts only after a downmix, boost, or other level change, the original encoded bitrate is not the first explanation to reach for. Follow the level through the processing path. Also distinguish sample peaks from peaks of the reconstructed waveform: values between sample instants can matter at conversion and output.

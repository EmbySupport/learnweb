    Audio Dithering

    Dither is a small noise signal added before audio is quantized. Adding noise can sound counterintuitive, but the purpose is to make rounding behave more predictably, especially for quiet material and fades.

    Without dither, a low-level signal can repeatedly land on the same few numerical levels. The resulting error follows the signal and can create distortion. Properly chosen dither prevents that pattern from dominating the result, at the cost of a little additional noise.

    Operation What happens at the quantizer
    Round without dither Error can depend on the signal
    Apply suitable dither, then round Signal-dependent rounding artifacts are replaced by controlled noise
    Use noise shaping The error spectrum is redistributed; the noise is not simply removed

    Triangular-probability-density dither is a common choice. The particular distribution and level matter; adding arbitrary background hiss is not equivalent to a properly designed dither process.

    When the choice matters

    Reducing a processed signal to a lower integer bit depth is a typical place to use dither. Gain changes or mixing can create fractional values even when the input started as integer PCM. A final conversion to 16-bit samples must decide how to round them.

    Merely copying samples without changing their values or precision does not require another rounding operation. Repeatedly adding dither to unchanged copies would only add noise.

    Dither also does not recreate a lost high-resolution recording. It allows low-level signal behavior to survive quantization statistically; an individual output sample still occupies one of the available levels.

    In Emby playback, precision conversion may occur in an audio decoder, processing stage, or output system. Do not assume that every conversion uses the same dither settings. The FFmpeg resampler exposes several choices, but their existence does not establish which a particular playback path uses. A smoothly decaying fade is the practical goal, not the appearance of a higher bit-depth label.

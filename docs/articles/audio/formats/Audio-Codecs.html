    Audio Codecs Explained

    A codec answers a practical question: what must the player understand to turn this track into sound? A media file can contain perfectly usable video while its soundtrack needs a decoder the client does not have.

    The name can refer to an encoded format or to software that implements it. Two AAC encoders may make different quality choices while producing tracks that the same AAC decoder can play.

    Three descriptions to keep separate

    Description Example What it identifies
    Container M4A or Matroska How tracks, timing, and tags are stored
    Audio codec AAC or FLAC How the audio data is represented
    Decoder implementation A player or system decoder The component that reconstructs sound

    A container extension alone cannot settle codec compatibility. An M4A file, for example, can contain AAC or Apple Lossless. The same extension can therefore describe two quite different audio streams.

    Compression and playback are different decisions

    Lossy codecs reduce data by allowing changes to the reconstructed signal. Lossless codecs preserve the encoded source samples exactly. Linear PCM stores sample values without audio compression.

    That distinction says little about which component decodes the track. A player can decode locally and send PCM to an amplifier, or pass a compatible encoded bitstream onward.

    When checking an Emby session, look at the codec, channel layout, and delivery method together. A client may accept a codec in stereo but require conversion for a multichannel presentation. A successful Direct Play session also does not establish that the receiver is receiving the original compressed format.

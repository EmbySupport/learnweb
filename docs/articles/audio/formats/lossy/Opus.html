    Opus Audio

    Opus handles speech and music in one lossy audio codec. Its design spans voice communication and full-band audio, making it useful for both conversational sound and stored media.

    It combines two approaches: linear prediction suited to speech, and transform coding suited to music and higher-frequency content. The encoder can use either approach or a hybrid arrangement according to the operating mode.

    Description What it means for Opus
    Bitrate The amount of encoded audio data
    Audio bandwidth The frequency range the stream actually carries
    Frame duration The time represented by a coded audio frame
    Channel mapping How decoded channels are assigned to the presentation

    These are separate properties. A high output sample rate does not prove that the encoder retained a full-band signal; it may be reconstructing a narrower-band source.

    Why 48 kHz appears so often

    Ogg Opus timing uses a 48,000-sample-per-second clock, regardless of the original capture rate or the decoder's chosen output rate. The input-rate field in its header is information about the source, not an instruction to interpret timestamps using that rate.

    Opus can be stored in Ogg and other containers. Mono and stereo streams are straightforward; multichannel presentations require the appropriate stream and channel mapping. Renaming an Ogg file does not change its codec.

    For an Emby library, an Opus track's packaging and presentation matter alongside the name. The low-delay design does not itself guarantee low latency through the rest of the player, operating system, or wireless output. Likewise, transcoding to another lossy codec is an additional conversion, not simply another way to unpack the existing Opus packets.

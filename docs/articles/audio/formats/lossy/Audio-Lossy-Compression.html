    Lossy Audio Compression

    Lossy audio compression makes a tradeoff: use fewer bits while keeping the audible result close to the source. The decoder reconstructs a signal that can sound convincing, but it cannot restore every original sample.

    Many codecs use knowledge of hearing. A loud sound can conceal a weaker sound nearby in frequency or time, so an encoder can spend fewer bits on information unlikely to be noticed. This is more nuanced than simply removing all high frequencies.

    Where the tradeoff becomes audible

    Material or constraint Possible effect at an insufficient bitrate
    Sharp attacks, such as percussion Smearing or a faint sound before the attack
    Cymbals, applause, or dense ambience Swirling, metallic, or watery texture
    Very restricted data rate Reduced bandwidth or less stable detail
    Repeated lossy conversions New artifacts added to an already altered signal

    These are possibilities, not a promise that every encoded track has audible damage. Codec design, encoder settings, source material, and listening conditions all influence the result. Equal bitrates also do not imply equal quality across different codecs.

    A higher-quality lossy version should normally be made from the original or a lossless master. Converting an existing low-bitrate file to a larger MP3 does not bring the discarded information back. Saving its decoded output as FLAC preserves that output, not the earlier source.

    During playback

    Emby may convert audio for a client's capabilities or available bandwidth. If both the source and delivery codec are lossy, this is another encoding generation. Keep that separate from ordinary decoding, which simply reconstructs the signal described by the existing bitstream.

    A compatible original track can avoid that extra conversion, but file size or a codec name alone cannot tell you whether a difference will be audible.

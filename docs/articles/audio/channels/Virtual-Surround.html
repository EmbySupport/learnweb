    Virtual Surround

    Virtual surround uses signal processing to suggest spatial positions with fewer physical speakers or with headphones.

    A headset marked “7.1” can reproduce virtual surround using one main driver per ear. The source positions are translated into directional cues, then combined into the two signals the headphones actually play.

    Virtual surround is this rendering job. It can operate after a conventional multichannel decoder or after a supported spatial decoder.

    Turn several speaker positions into two ear signals

    Consider front-left, center, and rear-right signals from a soundtrack. A headphone virtualizer treats each as a source at its assigned position:

    1. Choose the left-ear and right-ear filters associated with front left; filter that channel.
    2. Repeat for the center and rear right.
    3. Add all left-ear contributions together and all right-ear contributions together.
    4. Deliver the two resulting ear signals.

    The filters provide timing, level, and spectral relationships rather than merely making one channel louder. Binaural audio explains the directional cues and listener model.

    What reaches the virtualizer Available starting information
    Discrete multichannel audio Separate signals with channel positions
    Supported objects and metadata Components with spatial instructions
    Ordinary stereo Two source signals, possibly used for derived spatial processing
    An existing binaural render Ear signals that have already been spatialized

    Virtualizing an existing rear channel and deriving a new rear impression from stereo are different tasks. An upmixer can perform the second before virtualization, but does not give the processor access to original channels that were previously combined.

    Speakers require another acoustic strategy

    A speaker-based virtualizer has to account for each loudspeaker being heard by both ears. Some approaches shape or partly cancel the cross-ear paths to suggest positions beyond the physical speakers. Reflections, seating position, and listener movement affect that acoustic relationship.

    A method that works through headphones therefore cannot simply be assumed to give the same result through a soundbar. The input-to-ear path is different.

    Put the rendering at the intended stage

    During Emby playback, the system might deliver multichannel audio to a headphone renderer in the player or operating system. A different path might downmix to stereo earlier, leaving headset software only that smaller presentation.

    If a rear-channel identification sound disappears as a distinct position, inspect what the spatializer received. Keeping the source multichannel until a suitable renderer uses it preserves more positional information than first collapsing it into ordinary stereo.

    Also identify whether the track itself already contains a binaural render. Filtering those ear signals again changes their prepared timing and spectral cues; adding another effect is not simply adding more original spatial detail.

    A useful comparison uses the same source, roughly matched listening level, and one identified spatial-processing stage at a time. Listen for specific front/rear transitions and stable dialogue placement. That reveals what the renderer does to the input positions rather than just whether its mode sounds wider.

    Matrix Surround

    Matrix surround carries information for a surround presentation within fewer transmitted channels. The classic example is a two-channel signal designed to be decoded into front and surround outputs.

    The encoder combines channel contributions using chosen level and phase relationships. A matching decoder analyzes those relationships and steers sound toward the intended positions. Unlike discrete multichannel audio, the transmitted signal does not contain a separate, independently recoverable waveform for every output speaker.

    Label What it describes
    Lt/Rt: left total/right total Two channels prepared for matrix-surround decoding
    Lo/Ro: left only/right only A conventional stereo downmix without that matrix encoding
    Discrete multichannel Separate encoded signals for the defined channel roles

    Lt/Rt can be listened to as ordinary stereo. A compatible matrix decoder can then use its encoded relationships to produce a surround presentation. The chosen system matters: classic Dolby Surround and later Pro Logic II do not describe identical output arrangements.

    Two stored channels can be intentional

    An Emby media report showing two channels does not rule out matrix encoding. The spatial instructions are in the relationship between those channels rather than in six discrete channel streams. Preserving both channels and their relationship matters; taking one side or altering their phase independently can impair decoding.

    There is also a distinction between decoding an intentionally encoded matrix and applying an upmixer to an arbitrary stereo recording. The latter derives extra outputs from the available content, but cannot establish that the producer placed a particular sound in a separate source surround channel.

    Matrix decoding and virtual surround address different stages. A matrix decoder determines signals for surround positions; a virtualizer can try to reproduce those positions over headphones or a smaller speaker arrangement. Both can be involved in one session, but neither changes the file's original channel count.

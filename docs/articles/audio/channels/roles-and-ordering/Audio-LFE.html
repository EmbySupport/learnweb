    Low-Frequency Effects (LFE)

    A subwoofer plays bass from stereo music, even though the track has no LFE channel. During a 5.1 film, it may play both the main channels' bass and a separate effects signal. The subwoofer is a playback device; LFE is a source channel.

    Follow the two paths

    The main left, right, center, and surround channels can contain low frequencies. Bass management removes some of that low-frequency material from speakers configured to need assistance and redirects it to the bass output.

    Low-frequency effects (LFE) is a separately mixed, band-limited signal. It supplies additional low-frequency energy, such as selected film effects. ITU-R BS.775 gives its nominal upper boundary as 120 Hz.

    For an illustrative setup with an 80 Hz main-speaker crossover:

    Source content Relevant route
    A 60 Hz component in a main channel Can be redirected toward the subwoofer
    A 200 Hz component in that main channel Primarily remains in its main-speaker output
    A 100 Hz component in LFE Can reach the subwoofer through the separate LFE path

    This table illustrates the routing concepts. Real filters have gradual transition regions, rather than switching each frequency abruptly between outputs.

    An 80 Hz crossover therefore does not redefine LFE as an 80 Hz source channel. Crossover processing and LFE low-pass processing perform different jobs.

    Why “.1” is not a speaker count or a volume fraction

    In 5.1 or 7.1, “.1” denotes the limited-band effects signal. It is not one tenth of a main channel's amplitude. There are six or eight source signals respectively.

    One LFE signal can feed several calibrated subwoofers. A system can also route low frequencies to capable main speakers. Counting physical bass speakers therefore does not count the source's LFE channels.

    Understand the reproduction level convention

    For the broadcast/film convention described by BS.775, LFE is recorded with a −10 dB level offset and reproduced with compensation. Equal-level test signals give the LFE path a +10 dB acoustic output relationship within its low-frequency passband.

    That does not mean an arbitrary wideband meter reading should be ten decibels higher, or that every digital LFE waveform should be boosted manually. Bandwidth, calibration, decoder processing, and the delivery format's convention determine the intended result. The recommendation also notes formats using other LFE-offset conventions.

    This arrangement gives extra low-frequency effects headroom. Mixing compensated LFE with redirected bass must still avoid overloading the combined output.

    What remains after a stereo conversion

    A stereo downmix may omit the separate LFE signal while retaining bass already present in the main channels. Its result can still contain substantial bass. If a receiver subsequently redirects that stereo bass, the subwoofer can play again without LFE having been recreated.

    For an Emby source, keep those three observations separate: the selected track's LFE channel, any bass-management processing, and the bass signal finally reproduced. This identifies whether a missing effect, a crossover decision, or an output-level problem is actually responsible for the difference you hear.

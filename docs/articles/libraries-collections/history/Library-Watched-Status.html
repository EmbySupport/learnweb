    Watched status

    Watched status records a user’s played classification for a media item separately from its resume position or a complete record of viewed content.

    Watched status is a classification attached to a viewer and item: the system treats that item as played or unplayed. It drives choices such as unwatched filters and progress indicators.

    It is separate from the current resume point and from a detailed record of which passages were actually viewed. Emby's public user-item model exposes Played alongside PlaybackPositionTicks, PlayCount and LastPlayedDate. Those fields describe different aspects of state.

    Information Question it answers
    Played flag Is this item classified as played for this user?
    Resume position Where might this user continue?
    Play count What accumulated count does the system retain?
    Playback event log, if present Which events were recorded, and when?

    A catalogue item can be classified as seen because a viewer watched it elsewhere or explicitly marked it played. Emby's API includes a separate “mark played” operation; the classification need not originate from playing the local resource to its end.

    Final position is not viewed coverage

    Consider a fictional 60-minute programme. A viewer watches its first two minutes, seeks to minute 55, watches one minute and stops.

    The final position is 56:00, or about 93.33% of the duration. The viewed material totals three minutes, only 5% of the programme. A policy based on reaching a position near the end could classify this differently from a policy based on actual coverage.

    The example explains the difference; it is not Emby's completion threshold or a reproduction of its watched-state algorithm. A progress report describes a position and session state, not a proof that every intervening second was viewed.

    Classification can be an intentional correction

    Marking a film played can be useful when migrating an existing collection or recording that it was seen on another service. Marking it unplayed can express a new viewing intention. Neither action rewrites the movie's content.

    A manual correction should therefore be understood as a change to viewer state. It does not supply a forensic account of a screening, or a reliable measure of viewing time.

    A replay also introduces a new resume position without changing the historical fact that the person has seen the film before. How a product updates its played flag during replay depends on its state rules; the two concepts remain distinct.

    MPEG Transport Stream: TS and M2TS

    A television multiplex needs to keep delivering media even when receivers join halfway through. MPEG transport stream (TS) packages content in repeated, identifiable packets so a receiver can discover programmes and recover packet boundaries.

    A basic TS packet is 188 bytes, including its header. Its packet identifier, or PID, tells the reader which stream or signalling data the packet belongs to. One transport stream can carry several programmes, each with its own video, audio, and other components.

    Original schematic of stored packet framing. Header and payload are simplified; widths are not byte-accurate.

    PAT and PMT tables describe that programme structure. They are distinct from the codecs used to compress picture and sound. TS therefore does not mean that the video must be MPEG-2 Video.

    Why M2TS is slightly different

    Blu-ray stream files commonly use .m2ts. They wrap each 188-byte TS packet with an additional four-byte prefix, producing 192-byte stored units. The underlying transport packets retain their normal structure.

    A .ts recording and a Blu-ray .m2ts can consequently require different input handling even when they contain similar encoded video. Renaming one to the other does not add or remove the prefix.

    For an Emby recording or imported stream file, inspect the programme and track selection as well as the codecs. A large broadcast transport stream may contain components unrelated to the programme you want. Timing discontinuities or missing packets can also affect playback without changing the codec name.

    A remux into a file-oriented container can build more convenient navigation information, while copying suitable streams. It cannot recreate broadcast packets that were never received. Preserve the recording and check where a repeatable glitch occurs before treating it as a decoding issue.

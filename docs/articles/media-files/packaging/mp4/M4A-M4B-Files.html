    M4A and M4B File Conventions

    M4A and M4B usually describe audio-oriented members of the MP4 container family. The extensions help software recognize the intended use; they do not name an audio codec.

    M4A is common for music. M4B is commonly used for audiobooks, often with chapters and applications that remember a listening position. Those behaviours are implemented by the player and library, rather than guaranteed solely by the final letter of the filename.

    Property What the extension tells you
    Intended content M4A usually suggests audio; M4B usually suggests a book
    Audio encoding Must be read from the track; AAC and ALAC are possible examples
    Chapters Must actually be present in a supported chapter representation
    Resume behaviour Depends on the library and playback app

    An M4A can contain lossy AAC or lossless ALAC. Treating all M4A files as the same audio quality therefore overlooks a more important property than the extension.

    Book structure is separate from sound encoding

    A long recording can be one continuous audio track with chapter markers. The chapter boundaries do not imply that the encoded sound is stored as separate files or separately compressed tracks.

    Several chapter conventions exist in the MP4/QuickTime family. A conversion can preserve audio but omit the particular chapter information an app understands. Inspect the resulting chapter list and listening position behaviour separately from the audio properties.

    For Emby, use the appropriate library context and audiobook organization guidance. Renaming an audio file to .m4b does not add chapter titles, repair album metadata, or ensure that every client treats it as a book.

    When comparing a source with its converted copy, check encoding, complete duration, chapters, and descriptive tags. Those four facts explain much more than the filename convention.

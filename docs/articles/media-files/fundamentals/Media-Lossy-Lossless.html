    Lossy and Lossless Compression

    A lossless codec reconstructs exactly the digital samples supplied to its encoder. A lossy codec allows differences in the decoded result in exchange for a smaller representation, often concentrating its effort on changes people are less likely to notice.

    Both can make files smaller than uncompressed media. “Lossless” does not mean “uncompressed,” and “lossy” does not necessarily mean an obvious defect.

    Operation What is preserved?
    Lossless encode and decode The encoder's input samples
    Lossy encode and decode An approximation of those samples
    Copy encoded tracks into another container The copied encoded media, without another compression pass

    The source still matters

    Converting an MP3 recording to a lossless format preserves the samples decoded from that MP3. It cannot recover the information discarded when the MP3 was created. A lossless label describes the new compression step, not the history of the recording.

    The same principle applies to video compression. A lossless encode of a previously compressed film preserves that decoded picture, including any existing artifacts. Pixel-format conversions, scaling, or resampling before encoding may also change the material independently of the codec's lossless mode.

    For Emby, the source's compression type and the playback method answer separate questions. Direct Play can deliver a lossy source unchanged. A lossless source may need conversion for a particular app or output route.

    Repeated lossy re-encoding can introduce additional changes. If only the container needs changing, remuxing can often avoid that extra generation. Whether the tracks fit the destination container is a separate compatibility check.
